How to Write an SOP That Actually Gets Used

By Dr. Connor Robertson · May 14, 2026

Every business owner has been told to document their processes. Most try. Few succeed. The result is usually a Google Drive full of SOPs that nobody opens after the first week.

The problem is not that documentation does not work. The problem is that most SOPs are written wrong. They are either too vague to be useful or too detailed to be practical. They are created once and never updated. They are written for an audience that does not exist.

The Five Principles of Effective SOPs

1. Write for the Person Who Needs It Most

An SOP should be written for a competent person who has never done this specific task before. Not an expert. Not a complete beginner. Someone who understands the general domain but needs step-by-step guidance on this particular process.

If your SOP requires background knowledge that a new hire would not have, add a prerequisites section at the top. If it is so detailed that an experienced team member feels patronized, you have gone too far.

2. Use the Trigger-Steps-Outcome Format

Every SOP should answer three questions: When does this process start? What are the exact steps? How do I know it is done correctly?

  • Trigger: What event or condition initiates this process
  • Steps: The numbered sequence of actions, including decision points
  • Outcome: What the completed result looks like, including quality criteria

3. Include Decision Trees, Not Just Steps

Real processes have branches. The client says yes or no. The payment clears or it does not. The inventory is in stock or it is not. Your SOP needs to account for these forks. A simple if-then structure at each decision point prevents your team from getting stuck and coming to you for answers.

4. Make It Findable

The best SOP in the world is useless if nobody can find it when they need it. Use consistent naming conventions. Store SOPs in a single, searchable location. Link related SOPs to each other. And make sure your team knows where to look before they need to look.

5. Build in a Review Cycle

An SOP that was written six months ago and never updated is a liability, not an asset. Assign an owner to every SOP. Set a quarterly review date. And make it easy for anyone following the SOP to flag issues or suggest improvements in real time.

Start Today

Pick the one process that generates the most questions from your team. Document it using the trigger-steps-outcome format. Have someone follow it without your help. Fix what breaks. You have just created your first real SOP.
