Review an SOP Against the Task, Version and Observed Attempt

By Built to Run Editorial · Published August 1, 2026 · Updated October 30, 2026

A written procedure can describe a task without establishing that anyone has used it successfully. This article offers questions for reviewing an SOP against a particular task and a particular attempt. It does not certify a process, assess an employee or establish that a business can operate without its owner.

The prompts and fictional example below are original preparation material. They are not quotations from a book or a claim about a complete book framework. No universal page limit, step count or training schedule is proposed.

Identify the task and the version being reviewed

Ask: “Which task does this procedure cover, and which version are we looking at?” Record the task's stated starting condition, intended output and scope. A document about preparing a draft schedule may leave approval of schedule changes to a separate process. Keep that boundary visible rather than describing the document as covering all scheduling work.

Save the version used for any review or practice record. If the instructions change afterward, distinguish the earlier attempt from the revised document. A later edit does not change what the person had available during the earlier attempt.

Check what the instructions assume

“What input, tool, access or prior knowledge does this step assume?” is a useful question when a sentence such as “update the record” leaves the destination unclear. Identify the actual record and the action proposed. Viewing a record, drafting a change and applying the change are different tasks.

Use the organization's established access process. Knowing a file's location does not establish permission to open or modify it. A procedure should not rely on borrowing another person's credentials. If an access requirement remains unresolved, record the requirement and the accepted route for addressing it.

Read a decision point as a question with a boundary

For each branch, ask what information selects the path and what happens when that information is missing or conflicting. “If the request is approved” leaves a question if the document does not identify the approval record or the role that can interpret it. Do not replace missing approval with an assumption just to make the instructions appear complete.

Some situations may need a separate review rather than another instruction in this document. Record the unresolved case and the proposed contact. A person's name in a draft does not establish that they have accepted responsibility or will be available at the required time.

Describe an attempt by what was observed

An attempt record can identify the supplied input, procedure version, conditions, assistance, output and questions raised. If the usual worker supplied a missing definition or completed a step, retain that detail. The resulting output is evidence about that assisted attempt, not an unsupported attempt under different conditions.

Keep any exercise within the appropriate operational review and normal approvals. This article does not instruct readers to make a live change or withhold assistance. A successful sample attempt also leaves other inputs, exceptions and future conditions unresolved.

A fictional gap that a shorter document would not resolve

Suppose Mira uses procedure EX-SOP-4 to prepare a draft schedule from sample orders. The procedure says to flag orders marked “priority,” but the supplied sample has no priority field. Owen explains verbally which two sample orders to mark. Mira produces the draft, and no approver has reviewed it.

The record can say that Mira prepared a draft using EX-SOP-4 with Owen's explanation. It should retain the missing field definition and unreviewed output. Reducing the procedure to one page would not establish that the definition is correct, that the approver accepted the draft or that the same result would occur with another input. These names and identifiers are invented.

Separate a revision proposal from an accepted procedure

Write the gap, proposed wording and supporting basis together. For the fictional example, the next question might be where the existing priority definition is recorded. A proposal to add that reference remains a proposal until it passes the relevant review. Keep the previous version available so a reviewer can understand what changed.

Fewer questions or fewer edits alone do not establish that a procedure works. Ask what task was attempted, which conditions were observed and what remains unknown. A quiet document history does not reveal whether anyone used the document. For related reading, see questions about a proposed backup for critical work.

Common questions

Must every SOP fit on one page?

This article sets no universal page limit. Review whether the instructions cover the stated task, necessary context and unresolved exceptions without assuming that length establishes usefulness.

Does a completed sample output prove the procedure runs itself?

No. Describe the particular input, conditions, assistance and output reviewed. Other tasks and conditions remain unresolved.

Should verbal assistance be omitted from the attempt record?

No. Retain it so an assisted attempt is not represented as an unsupported one.

Does an unchanged procedure mean nobody reads it?

The edit history alone does not establish use or nonuse. Look for records of the particular task and attempt being reviewed.
