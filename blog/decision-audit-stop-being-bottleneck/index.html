Review a Decision Log Before Changing Ownership or Authority

By Built to Run Editorial · Published June 20, 2026 · Updated October 30, 2026

A decision log can help organize questions about how work was routed. It does not, by itself, establish why someone asked for help or whether authority should change. Begin with the particular request, the records available at that time and the action that remained unresolved.

The review prompts and fictional example below are original editorial material. They are not a reproduced book framework, an assessment of an actual team or a promise of faster decisions. This article sets no universal observation period, expected request count or deadline for an operating result.

Define the request before assigning a cause

Ask: “What action was the person asking about?” A request to locate a document is different from a request to interpret it, approve an exception or apply a change. Keep the actual wording where appropriate and distinguish the question asked from your later summary of it.

Record the relevant task and context without inferring a person's motive or capability. An escalation may raise questions about information, scope, ownership or authority, but the log alone does not determine which explanation applies. Leave several possibilities open when the available record does not distinguish them.

Separate receipt, response and resolution

A record can identify when a question was received, when someone responded and what the response actually did. An acknowledgment, request for clarification and completed decision are separate events. If the eventual resolution is unknown, retain that status instead of using the first reply as the completion time.

Use the time basis stated in the record. A date without a clock time cannot support a minute-level response interval. An elapsed interval also does not show how much active work occurred, what other prerequisites were pending or why the interval arose. Treat those as further questions rather than diagnoses.

Identify the information available when the question arose

Ask which procedure, policy, input or prior decision the person had available. Preserve the version and relevant scope. A reference added later does not establish that it was accessible, applicable or understood at the earlier time.

If a document appears relevant, compare its stated task with the request. A general guide may leave an exception unresolved. An unchanged document may have been used or may not have been used; edit history does not settle that question. Record the gap precisely before proposing another document or a revision.

Keep authority separate from a proposed reassignment

Identify what the available records say about the role permitted to take the particular action. A job title, frequent response or name in a draft does not by itself establish accepted responsibility for that decision. Viewing information, preparing a recommendation and approving or applying a change remain different activities.

A proposed change to decision ownership should remain labeled as a proposal. State the scope, conditions and unresolved prerequisites for the appropriate reviewer to consider. This article supplies no approval threshold, financial limit or permission to extend authority. Any actual change needs the organization's applicable review and acceptance.

A fictional log with two different unanswered questions

Suppose an invented entry EX-DEC-4 says Mira asked Owen where to find the current schedule guide. Owen supplied a link. The entry does not say whether Mira opened the guide, whether it covered the requested exception or who could approve that exception.

A later summary saying Owen approved the schedule would add a decision that the record does not contain. A summary saying Mira lacked skill would add an employee assessment. The supported next questions concern the guide version, the exception's scope and the actual approval record. These names and identifiers are fictional.

Review patterns without converting counts into explanations

Several entries with a similar label may concern different actions or conditions. Before grouping them, ask what makes the requests comparable. Retain individual references so a repeated topic is not mistaken for repeated identical work.

Fewer logged questions in a later period would still leave questions about what work occurred, what was recorded and whether requests moved elsewhere. A count alone does not establish independence, better decisions or a successful intervention. Describe the observed records and keep the proposed explanation separate.

Turn the review into a bounded next question

For each unresolved entry, name the missing information and the proposed reviewer or next step. Confirm whether a named person has accepted the request. A drafted action list is different from assigned work, and assigned work is different from a completed or reviewed result.

If the issue concerns a written procedure, read questions for reviewing an SOP against its task and observed attempt. For a proposed alternate worker, read questions about a backup for critical work. Each review should retain the particular scope and evidence rather than promising an owner-independent outcome.

Common questions

Does a decision log identify the cause of an escalation?

Not by itself. Retain the request, available information and unresolved action, then ask which evidence would distinguish possible explanations.

Is the first response the same as a completed decision?

No. An acknowledgment or request for clarification may leave the decision unresolved. Keep receipt, response and resolution separate.

Does naming a different decision owner change their authority?

A proposed assignment is not an accepted authority change. Confirm the actual scope, applicable review and acceptance.

Do fewer logged questions prove greater operating independence?

A count alone does not establish what work occurred, what was recorded or why requests changed. Keep the observed records separate from the proposed explanation.
