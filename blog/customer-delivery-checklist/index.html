Service commitment and operational handoff

Customer Delivery Checklist: From Commitment to Handoff

Connect the service that was agreed, the person responsible for delivery, the evidence of completion, and any exceptions that remain open.

Published September 23, 2026Reviewed November 22, 2026By Dr. Connor Robertson

Use this checklist for the handoff between a service commitment and the team responsible for performing it. The record should let the next person find the agreed scope, see what is ready, recognize an exception, and know which evidence will support the next status. It should not turn an internal assumption into a customer promise.

Review eight parts of the handoff record

Choose a defined service or deliverable and keep a reference to the current source record. Write down unresolved items rather than marking the entire handoff complete because a form was filled in.

RecordWhat to establishEvidence to connect
1. Commitment and versionThe service or deliverable, current agreed scope, exclusions, and the record being used.The relevant proposal, order, statement of work, or other authorized instruction and its version.
2. Delivery ownerThe person accountable for coordinating the work, with a backup or escalation contact where appropriate.The assignment and handoff acknowledgment; a department name alone may leave responsibility unclear.
3. Required inputsThe information, materials, approved access, customer dependencies, and qualifications needed to start.The actual input records and any missing prerequisite, with an owner and next action.
4. Timing and capacityWhich dates are requested, proposed, or confirmed and what those dates depend on.The current schedule and capacity check; distinguish a desired date from a confirmed commitment.
5. Completion evidenceThe expected output, who checks it, and where the check is recorded.The completed work and its review record; a task-status label is not a substitute for the output.
6. Exceptions and changesWhat differs from the expected path and who can decide the response.A separate issue or change record linked to the original commitment, with its status visible.
7. Customer communicationThe authorized contact, channel, next update, and information the recipient needs.The relevant communication reference; use the organization’s normal handling for customer information.
8. Handoff and follow-upWhat is being passed on, who receives it, and which open items remain with a named owner.The handoff record, delivery or receipt evidence appropriate to the workflow, and the next review.

Keep readiness, performance, and receipt separate

Define the statuses the workflow uses. A service can be scheduled while a prerequisite remains unresolved. A file can be prepared before it is delivered. A notification can be sent before the recipient has confirmed receipt. Do not assume that changing one status establishes the others.

Before marking work ready, check the prerequisites that matter to this service, the responsible person, and the next decision. If a required input is missing, identify the gap and its consequence for the plan. Let the authorized owner decide whether to hold, revise, or proceed within the applicable boundaries.

For examples of these distinctions, read the three fictional customer-delivery records. That companion page applies the checklist to specific situations rather than repeating the checklist itself.

Separate an exception from a new commitment

Record what changed, when it was identified, which source record it affects, and who can decide the response. Keep the original scope visible. A request for additional work should not silently become part of the original task, and an unresolved issue should not disappear because the delivery team completed a different step.

  1. Describe the observation using the actual record: a missing input, conflicting instruction, changed request, access problem, or output that did not meet the specified check.
  2. Name the responsible reviewer and the decision needed. Avoid giving the receiving person authority that has not been assigned.
  3. Record the effect on the current plan and the customer update required under the applicable process. Distinguish a proposed response from a confirmed one.
  4. Keep the issue open until the specified evidence exists. If a separate change is approved, link its version and decision to the work record.

For repeated approval requests, use the delegation exception log. For unclear instructions, use the SOP review checklist.

Close the handoff with a traceable record

Identify the delivered item or service, the version, the checks performed, the receiving party or role, and the evidence appropriate to the workflow. Keep any separate unresolved issue visible with its next owner. Use status labels consistently; do not infer a formal approval or acceptance merely from an internal upload or an automated message.

When work is handed to another team, state what they are receiving and what they still need to decide or perform. A useful review can conclude “ready for the next step with this named dependency” rather than making an unsupported claim that the entire customer engagement is complete.

Questions for the delivery review

Who owns the handoff?

Name the coordinating person and the people responsible for particular decisions. Responsibility for sending a file, checking its content, and deciding a scope change may belong to different roles.

What if the customer asks for something different?

Record the request against the current commitment and route the decision to the authorized owner. Keep the new request’s status separate from the existing deliverable.

What should the checklist measure?

Use observations that support the decision, such as unresolved prerequisites, missed checks, rework, or unclear handoffs. Define the observation period and source. Do not claim that completing a checklist proves customer satisfaction or reduces errors by a particular percentage.

How should complaints affect the workflow?

Preserve the complaint or issue record, identify the work it concerns, and follow the responsible review process. Use repeated findings to examine the underlying workflow, without treating every complaint as proof of the same cause.

Source and scope

NIST’s Baldrige criteria commentary discusses understanding customer requirements and using complaint information for improvement. The checklist and record format here are original teaching tools. They do not represent a Baldrige assessment, endorsement, or a determination about any specific service agreement.
