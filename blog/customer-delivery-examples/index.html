Customer Delivery Examples: Readiness, Scope Changes, and Receipt

Work through three different handoff problems without confusing a missing prerequisite, a changed request, and evidence of receipt.

Published September 23, 2026Reviewed November 22, 2026By Dr. Connor Robertson

These invented examples show how to read a delivery record and identify the next evidence needed. They are not client stories, measured improvements, or promises about service outcomes. No customer communication or system change is performed by the examples. Use the customer-delivery checklist for the reusable handoff structure.

Example 1: a scheduled visit with an unresolved prerequisite

The record: A fictional service team has a proposed site-visit date and the correct address. Its work requires an access arrangement. The record names the person who can confirm that arrangement, but contains no confirmation.

The tempting conclusion: “The appointment is scheduled, so the work is ready.” That conclusion skips a prerequisite. It also does not establish that the visit has occurred or that any service output exists.

The review: Keep the proposed date, the missing access information, the decision owner, and the next contact or evidence request visible. The responsible person decides how the missing prerequisite affects the plan. Avoid silently treating an unconfirmed arrangement as a completed check.

The next record: After the necessary information arrives, connect it to the schedule decision. Later, retain the actual visit record and completion check separately. A confirmed appointment is evidence about scheduling, not evidence that the service was performed.

Example 2: an extra request during report preparation

The record: A fictional team is preparing the report described in its current instruction. The customer asks for an additional analysis. The request is recorded, but the responsible owner has not decided whether or how to change the work.

The tempting conclusion: “The customer asked, so the new item is now part of the current delivery.” That merges a request with a decision. It can also hide whether the original report is ready, delayed, or waiting for direction.

The review: Keep two linked records: the existing deliverable and the requested change. State the current version, the proposed difference, who can decide it, and which dates or inputs may be affected. Do not label the requested analysis approved merely because someone captured it in a task list.

The next record: Link the authorized change decision, or the decision to keep the original scope, to the relevant versions and communication record. The delivery status should then describe the actual work being handed over.

Example 3: an internal upload without a complete handoff record

The record: A fictional team has reviewed a digital file and placed it in an internal folder. Its delivery record does not show the authorized recipient’s access, the delivery route, or the receipt evidence the workflow expects.

The tempting conclusion: “The file exists, so the customer has received it.” Preparation and internal storage do not establish that handoff.

The review: Identify the approved recipient or role, the intended version, the authorized sharing method, and the relevant checks. Follow the normal process for access and customer information. Do not broaden permissions simply to make a handoff appear finished.

The next record: Connect the delivery and any receipt evidence to the reviewed version. If the applicable process requires a separate review or acceptance step, keep that step separate. Refer uncertainty about the required status to the responsible person.

Use the cases as a team review exercise

  1. For each case, identify the fact supported by the current record and the tempting conclusion that goes further than the evidence.
  2. Name the missing evidence and the person responsible for the next decision. Do not fill in a favorable outcome.
  3. Write the narrow status the record can support now. Preserve the separate scheduling, preparation, delivery, receipt, and review steps that apply.
  4. Compare a similar real workflow only with appropriate handling of customer information. Use a sanitized teaching copy when discussing examples outside the authorized team.
  5. Use the blank handoff checklist and CSV to record an actual review, including open items and next actions.

The cases have different causes: a missing prerequisite, an undecided change, and incomplete handoff evidence. Giving each one the same generic “follow up” task would lose the decision the next person needs to make.

What these examples do and do not establish

The examples illustrate how to keep a record’s conclusion within its evidence. They do not establish that a real service was delivered, that a customer was satisfied, or that an agreement was fulfilled. Use the actual service instructions and responsible reviewers for those conclusions.
