Procedure review and controlled test

Standard Operating Procedure Checklist: Write, Test, and Maintain an SOP

Check whether a procedure tells the intended user when to start, what to do, when to stop, and how to demonstrate completion. Then test the written version under appropriate supervision.

Published September 23, 2026Reviewed November 22, 2026By Dr. Connor Robertson

A standard operating procedure describes how a defined task is performed. A checklist helps review or carry out that procedure; it does not replace the instructions, the user’s qualifications, or required approvals. A document can look complete while leaving the next person unable to find a system, recognize an exception, or verify that the result is correct.

Define the task and the intended user

Start with one workflow, not “everything the department does.” Name its start event, expected output, and boundaries. Identify the user’s assumed training, the authority they have, and the conditions that require a supervisor or specialist. A procedure intended for a trained coordinator may reasonably differ from a training document for a new hire.

Use the SOP-writing article when drafting the first version. Use this checklist to review a particular draft and record what was actually tested. Use the backup-person test for a more focused handoff exercise.

Review the SOP against eight practical checks

For each item, record the document section, the evidence reviewed, the gap, and who will resolve it. “Not applicable” needs an explanation. “Unknown” remains an open item; it is not a completed check.

CheckWhat the procedure should make clearEvidence to retain
1. Identity and ownershipThe document title, version, responsible owner, approval status, and location of the current copy.The reviewed version and the named reviewer; a draft should not look like an approved instruction.
2. Start, scope, and outputThe event that starts the task, what is included, what is excluded, and the expected result.A realistic input and an example of the expected output, with sensitive information removed from teaching copies.
3. PrerequisitesRequired qualifications, tools, source records, approved access, and conditions that must exist before work begins.A check that the intended user can reach the required resources through the normal approved process.
4. Steps and decisionsAn ordered sequence with actions, decision points, and references specific enough for the intended user.The section used in the test and any step where the user had to guess or obtain missing instructions.
5. Exceptions and stop conditionsWhat is outside the routine path, when to stop, and who can authorize the next action.An exception scenario and the escalation route; do not test it by creating a real harmful event.
6. Completion checksThe result to inspect, acceptance criteria, reviewer where needed, and record that demonstrates completion.The output and its check, not merely a marked checklist or a statement that the task was done.
7. Handoff and recordsWho receives the result, where the record is stored, and what information must accompany it.A named destination and the record reference; “notify the team” may be too ambiguous.
8. Change and reviewHow errors, process changes, and obsolete references are reported and incorporated into a controlled revision.The change reason, reviewer, current version link, and disposition of earlier versions.

Test the written procedure without hiding the gaps

  1. Choose a safe test. Define the task, input, intended user, current version, expected output, supervision, and stop conditions. Use a simulation when a live test could affect people, money, customer commitments, security, or regulated work.
  2. Check prerequisites first. Confirm appropriate training, approved access, and required source records. Missing prerequisites are findings; do not bypass them to finish the exercise.
  3. Observe the attempt. Record where the user paused, guessed, used another reference, or needed help. If a supervisor intervenes, retain that observation rather than counting the attempt as independent execution.
  4. Inspect the output. Compare the actual result with the stated completion check. A fast attempt that produces the wrong result is not a successful test.
  5. Classify and resolve findings. Distinguish a document gap from a training, access, authority, equipment, or capacity problem. Assign a response and a responsible reviewer.
  6. Retest the relevant change. Preserve the earlier finding and the new evidence. State exactly what was tested and what remained outside the exercise.

A walkthrough can reveal unclear language, but it may not demonstrate execution. A test of one routine case may not cover exceptions or rare events. Keep the conclusion as narrow as the evidence.

Common mistakes in an SOP checklist

  • Reviewing a title instead of a version. Two copies with the same name can contain different instructions.
  • Using “clear to the author” as the only test. The author may supply context that the receiving person does not have.
  • Replacing authority with a checkbox. A tick does not grant permission to approve a payment, change a contract, or access a restricted system.
  • Leaving the output subjective. “Handle appropriately” does not identify what will be checked or by whom.
  • Silently helping during the test. Record assistance so the next review can distinguish a usable document from undocumented support.
  • Publishing a revision without updating references. An approved file can remain ineffective if users are directed to an obsolete form or shortcut.

Maintain the procedure after the test

Keep the current procedure, approval record, review findings, and related forms connected. Record why a revision occurred and which instructions or references changed. Make earlier versions identifiable so someone can understand which instructions applied to an earlier event.

Choose review points appropriate to the workflow, such as a process or system change, recurring error, changed responsibility, or an agreed review date. This guide does not prescribe a universal review interval. If a change affects qualifications, authority, safety, or compliance, involve the responsible people before treating the revised instructions as ready for use.

Questions for the review meeting

Is an SOP the same as a checklist?

No. A procedure explains the task and its decisions. A checklist can prompt execution or review of selected points. It should refer to the relevant procedure and version rather than pretend to contain every instruction.

How detailed should an SOP be?

Detailed enough for the defined, appropriately trained user to perform the scoped task and recognize exceptions. Test that assumption. Add detail where the evidence shows a gap rather than setting a word-count target.

Can software or an AI draft approve a procedure?

A draft or suggested step still needs review by people with appropriate knowledge and authority. Check the actual systems, access, source documents, exceptions, and completion evidence. A fluent description is not evidence that the process has been tested.

When is the review complete?

When the responsible reviewer can identify the version, scope, findings, supporting output, unresolved items, and next action. A completed review can still conclude that the procedure needs revision or that further testing is required.

Source and scope

EPA’s guidance on preparing standard operating procedures is a reference for documenting and reviewing procedures in a quality system. Its environmental-information context does not make this business worksheet an EPA standard, approval, or compliance assessment. The review structure and fictional administrative records here are original teaching tools; use the requirements that apply to the actual work.
