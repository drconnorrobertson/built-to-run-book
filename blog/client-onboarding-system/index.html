Review a Client Onboarding Handoff Against Accepted Commitments

By Built to Run Editorial · Published September 12, 2026 · Updated October 30, 2026

A client onboarding note can connect what was discussed, what was accepted and what remains to be arranged. To review it, start with the specific engagement and document version. A template describes a proposed process; it does not establish what a particular client was promised or what the team has delivered.

Keep commitments attached to their source

For each item, identify the relevant agreement, accepted proposal, message or other source and its date. Distinguish a client’s stated goal from a deliverable somebody agreed to provide. A desired result, a sales discussion and an accepted commitment can involve the same subject while carrying different meanings.

If two sources differ, record both versions and the question that needs clarification. Do not silently choose the more convenient wording. A missing attachment or ambiguous phrase can remain an open question for the responsible participant. This article does not interpret an agreement or determine which source governs a dispute.

Identify the recipient and accepted responsibility

A handoff needs more than a name in a field. Which work has the receiving person accepted? Which information can they access, and which inputs are still outstanding? Separate a proposed assignment from acceptance and acceptance from completed work. Record the agreed scope rather than assuming that one person owns every subsequent question.

Keep a proposed contact distinct from permission to contact them or share material. Ask the responsible engagement participants about access, communication scope and approvals. An internal checklist does not authorize an external message, disclosure or change to a client commitment.

Review dates without inventing a universal schedule

Label each date by its role: requested, proposed, agreed, started or completed. A kickoff invitation does not establish attendance. A requested delivery date is different from an accepted deadline. Record the source of the date and any dependency that remains unresolved.

For a milestone, specify the deliverable and the evidence that would demonstrate its stated completion. An internal status label can show that a task was marked done without showing what was sent, received or accepted. Preserve those differences instead of treating every checked box as the same kind of progress.

Make missing inputs visible

List the input needed, who was asked, when the request was made and whether a response has arrived. “Client information pending” may hide several unrelated questions. A more useful note identifies the particular file or decision needed for the named task.

Do not infer a reason for silence from a missing response. A note can establish that a request is unanswered within the reviewed record; it cannot establish unwillingness, dissatisfaction or the cause of a delay. Add a later explanation with its source rather than rewriting the earlier record as if it were known at the time.

A fictional onboarding handoff

Suppose an invented engagement EX-ENG-6 includes a draft reporting outline. Mira records a proposed kickoff for June 12, while Owen accepts responsibility for preparing an example once a sample file arrives. The client has not confirmed the date and the file is still requested. The note can say “kickoff proposed; preparation accepted subject to input; sample file pending.” It cannot say “onboarding complete” or predict the client’s experience.

If the file later arrives, record its identifier and version. If Owen prepares an example, identify what was produced and whether the relevant recipient has reviewed it. The new event may resolve one question while leaving the proposed meeting or acceptance criteria open. Mira, Owen, the engagement and dates in this example are fictional.

Review the handoff into ongoing work

When responsibility changes, record the accepted scope, remaining items, available records and proposed next review. A transition label is not evidence that every item is complete. Identify who has accepted each open question and which items are still awaiting an assignment or response.

These are original editorial prompts about reviewing records. They are not a book quotation, a chapter method, a report of a live client engagement or a professional assessment. No claim is made that a template guarantees consistency, retention, satisfaction or independent operation. For a separate document-review task, see reviewing an SOP against a task and observed attempt.

Common questions

Does a completed checklist establish that a client accepted delivery?

No. Record internal completion, delivery, receipt and acceptance as distinct states supported by their own evidence.

Should every engagement use the same onboarding dates?

Identify the actual commitments and dependencies for the engagement. A template date does not establish an agreed deadline.

Does a named handoff recipient own every open item?

Record the scope the recipient accepted and identify any items that remain unassigned or unanswered.

Can a missing response establish why an onboarding item is delayed?

No. Record the unanswered request and relevant sources without inferring a motive or cause.
