Delegation Without Constant Approvals: Build an Exception Log

Turn repeated approval requests into clear decision rights, evidence requirements, escalation triggers, and a weekly exception review.

If employees ask the founder to approve the same decision every week, the problem may be a missing boundary rather than a lack of confidence. A task can be delegated while the authority to decide remains unclear. The result is work that moves forward only when the owner answers a message.

Start with real interruptions

For one week, record each approval request. Capture the decision, customer or project affected, available evidence, reason for escalation, and time spent waiting. Do not write a company-wide authority policy from memory. Look for repeated decisions whose inputs and risks are already understood.

Separate requests caused by unclear rules from requests caused by missing information, missing access, or a genuine unusual event. A team cannot use delegated authority if the person still cannot see the account history or issue the approved refund.

Define a decision boundary

For each recurring decision, name one accountable role, the conditions in which it can decide, required evidence, and escalation triggers. Monetary limits may help, but they are rarely sufficient by themselves. A small refund involving a threatened legal claim can require different handling from a routine service credit.

For example, a fictional service team might allow its lead to issue a routine credit within a stated limit when the service record confirms an incomplete visit. A request outside that evidence pattern goes to the manager. The actual amount, conditions, and exceptions must fit the business's economics and obligations.

Make escalation useful

Replace messages saying “What should I do?” with a short decision packet: situation, verified facts, available options, recommendation, deadline, and consequence of waiting. The manager can then decide or clarify the rule without reconstructing the case.

Every escalation should have a fallback when the decision-maker is unavailable. That fallback might be a named alternate or a pre-agreed pause. Do not leave an employee choosing between an unauthorized commitment and an undefined wait.

Review exceptions, not every routine action

Hold a short weekly review of exceptions. Ask which cases revealed a missing rule, which were handled correctly, and which need training or access changes. Update the operating procedure with a version date and notify the people using it.

Track repeat-escalation count, waiting time, corrections, and customer outcomes. A falling approval count is useful only if service quality and financial control remain acceptable. Keep visibility through records and sampling rather than forcing the founder back into every routine decision.

Run a two-week pilot

Choose one frequent decision with manageable consequences. Write the boundary, test it on recent cases, and let the team use it for two weeks. Review exceptions before widening authority. This creates evidence about the process without requiring a complete organizational redesign.
